## 1.1 Background of the study

Fish is the single most important source of animal protein in the Bangladeshi diet and the backbone of a livelihood system that engages millions. In 2023–24 the country produced **50.18 lakh metric tonnes (5.02 million MT)** of fish, of which inland open-water capture contributed 14.11 lakh MT (28.13 %), inland closed-water culture 29.78 lakh MT (59.34 %) and **marine capture 6.29 lakh MT (0.63 million MT, 12.53 %)** (DoF, 2024). The marine sub-sector is small in volume relative to aquaculture but disproportionate in economic and nutritional weight: it is the only part of the sector that cannot be expanded by pond construction, it supplies the high-value table fish that dominate urban retail, and it sustains a coastal population of fishers, traders, transporters, ice-plant workers and market labourers.

The marine sub-sector is also the part under the greatest pressure. Marine production **fell by 7.47 % year-on-year in 2023–24**, from 6.79 lakh MT to 6.29 lakh MT, with the industrial trawl fleet down 21.4 % (DoF, 2024). The fleet is dominated by small-scale operators: roughly **28,575 artisanal and mechanised vessels** produce 5.14 lakh MT (81.74 % of the marine catch) alongside **237 industrial trawlers** producing 1.15 lakh MT (DoF, 2024). Fishing remains confined to waters shallower than 200 m even though Bangladesh holds sovereign rights over **118,813 km²** of the Bay of Bengal including an exclusive economic zone, leaving pelagic and deep-sea resources largely untapped (DoF, 2024).

Hilsa (*Tenualosa ilisha*) — the national fish, a geographical-indication product and the largest single-species fishery in the country — straddles both worlds. It yielded **5.29 lakh MT in 2023–24, about 10.55 % of all fish production by volume**, itself a 7.33 % decline on the previous year (DoF, 2024). Historically marine waters have supplied a little over half of Bangladesh's hilsa catch (55 % in 2017–18, FRI), which places the Chattogram coast and its markets at the centre of the country's most politically and nutritionally sensitive fishery.

**Chattogram is the hinge.** It is Bangladesh's principal marine fish landing and trading hub, where the catch from the Bay of Bengal coast is landed, auctioned, and redistributed both within the metropolitan retail system and onward to inland wholesale centres. What distinguishes Chattogram as a research site is that the *entire* marketing chain is present within one urban economy: beach landing points where first sale occurs, wholesale assembly yards (*arats*) where commission agents intermediate, intermediary traders who move consignments, and dense neighbourhood retail markets where the urban consumer buys. A survey designed inside a single city can therefore observe both ends of the price chain — what the fisher receives and what the consumer pays — in a single frame and on the same days, which is not possible when landing and terminal market are hundreds of kilometres apart.

The chain through which that fish travels is conventionally described in four tiers. The **aratdar** operates the physical *arat*, provides trading space, weighing and auction coordination, and earns commission or lease income on the lots routed through his premises — he is a commission-earning facilitator, not the owner of the fish. The **bepari/faria** is a mobile wholesaler who buys at the landing or an upstream yard, transports, and sells on through arats. The **khuchra** vendor sells to the final consumer from a fixed stall or vending spot. Between the fisher and the consumer's plate, each of these actors adds a margin and incurs a cost, and the difference between the producer price and the consumer price — the **price spread** — is the sum of those margins.

## 1.2 Statement of the problem

Every taka the consumer spends on marine fish is divided between the fisher who caught it and the intermediaries who moved, iced, financed and retailed it. The central question of fish marketing research is how that division falls, and whether the intermediaries' share is a payment for services rendered or a rent extracted from an opaque, fragmented and credit-dependent market.

Three features of the Chattogram system make this question acute.

**First, the spread is large and its composition is undocumented for this city.** Published work reports producer shares ranging from roughly 51 % in long multi-stage chains to 74–77 % in short coastal ones, with marketing margins of 21–30 % typical of Bangladesh fish chains (Chapter 2). Where Chattogram sits on that range has not been established by a published multi-market, four-stratum decomposition. Without that measurement, neither the fisher's position nor the intermediary's defence can be assessed on evidence.

**Second, the costs that generate the margin are concentrated in perishable-goods infrastructure that is visibly deficient at the point of landing.** Ice, cold storage, drainage, sanitation and reliable electricity determine how much fish is lost between the boat and the stall. Where these fail, the loss is priced into the margin. The observable condition of Chattogram's landing-linked markets suggests that the two markets where the producer price is set are also the two least well served — a claim this study tests with a structured observation checklist.

**Third, the market is information-poor and credit-dependent.** Prices are negotiated bilaterally, vary within the day and between adjacent markets, and are not publicly posted. Working capital moves largely as trade credit rather than through formal channels. In such a market, price dispersion across markets is not necessarily arbitrageable by participants, and the consumer's ability to discipline retail pricing is weak.

Against this background the study asks, concretely: **how much of the consumer's taka reaches the fisher in Chattogram's marine fish markets; who captures the remainder; and is that remainder justified by the costs incurred and the services performed?**

## 1.3 Justification and significance of the study

Four considerations justify the study.

**(i) It addresses a documented empirical gap.** Recent marine-fish margin studies in Bangladesh are overwhelmingly anchored either on a single landing point (Cox's Bazar, Bagerhat) or on Dhaka's terminal markets. Chattogram — the country's principal marine fish trading hub — is comparatively under-represented in published margin decomposition work, despite offering the methodological advantage that first-sale and consumer prices can be observed within one survey frame (Chapter 2, §2.5).

**(ii) It supplies a chain-anchored, internally additive decomposition.** Many published margin estimates are built from prices collected at different times, places or tiers, so their components do not sum to the whole. This study fixes the producer price at the landing-linked markets, the retail anchor at the price consumers actually paid (verified against exit slips), and requires the three segment margins to telescope to the total spread and to sum with the producer's share to 100 % of the consumer's taka. Those identities are checked automatically at the end of every run (Chapter 4, §4.10).

**(iii) It normalises marketing cost to the kilogram for *every* stratum, not only retailers.** The conventional practice of costing the retail tier alone understates the chain's cost base and inflates the apparent profitability of the wholesale tiers. Doing it for all three strata is what makes a net marketing margin and a Shepherd-type efficiency index computable at all (Methodology §3.7.1–3.7.4).

**(iv) It validates quoted margins against an independent source.** Trader self-reports of buy and sell prices are the weak point of any margin survey, because the respondent has an interest in the answers. This study links buyer and seller forms through a pair identifier and screens the matched differences with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and independently checks retail selling prices against consumer exit slips (Methodology §3.3.4).

The findings are of practical relevance to the Department of Fisheries and to market committees responsible for landing-centre infrastructure, to organisations promoting digital payments and price information services, and to researchers seeking a benchmark for coastal fish marketing in Bangladesh.

## 1.4 Objectives of the study

**Broad objective.** To analyse the marketing system, channel structure, marketing costs, margins and price spread of commercially important marine fish species in the Chattogram metropolitan region, and to estimate the fisher's share of the consumer's taka.

**Specific objectives.**

1. To describe the socio-economic profile and business scale of the marketing intermediaries (aratdars, bepari/faria, and khuchra retailers) and of purchasing consumers in the study markets.
2. To identify the marketing channels through which marine fish reaches the consumer in Chattogram, and the pattern of product and payment flow along those channels.
3. To estimate the marketing costs incurred at each segment of the channel, expressed per kilogram handled.
4. To estimate the marketing margin and net profit of each intermediary category per kilogram, and to compare them across strata.
5. To estimate the price spread, gross marketing margin, net marketing margin, producer's share and marketing efficiency for the focal species, and to compare these across species.
6. To examine whether prices of the same species differ across the six study markets, and where they do, to identify which market pairs drive the difference.
7. To examine the adoption of payment modes — cash, mobile financial services and credit — across actor classes.
8. To identify the principal marketing problems reported by traders and to document the physical infrastructure of the study markets.
9. To draw conclusions and recommend measures for improving the efficiency and transparency of the marine fish marketing system in Chattogram.

## 1.5 Research questions

The objectives translate into six questions that carry a designated statistical test each (Methodology Table 3.6):

| # | Research question | Objective | Designated test |
|---|---|---|---|
| RQ1 | Do prices of the same species differ across the six study markets? | 6 | Kruskal–Wallis H, Dunn post-hoc with Holm control |
| RQ2 | Do marketing margins differ across trader strata? | 4 | Mann–Whitney U per stratum pair |
| RQ3 | Are self-reported buy-versus-sell quotations internally consistent? | 4 | Wilcoxon signed-rank on matched pairs |
| RQ4 | Does payment-mode adoption differ across actor classes? | 7 | Chi-square, with exact and likelihood-ratio fallbacks |
| RQ5 | Is per-kilogram marketing cost associated with segment profit? | 3, 4 | Spearman rank correlation |
| RQ6 | What share of the consumer's taka reaches the producer, and how does it decompose across segments? | 5 | Descriptive decomposition with additivity checks |

Objectives 1, 2, 7 and 8 are addressed descriptively (Tables 1, 2, 2b, 5, 6, 7, 8); RQ1–RQ5 carry the inferential battery.

## 1.6 Hypotheses

Consistent with the two-sided, distribution-free plan of Methodology §3.8, each research question is posed in null form:

- **H₀₁:** The distribution of prices for a given species is the same across the six study markets.
- **H₀₂:** The distribution of per-kilogram margins is the same across the three trader strata.
- **H₀₃:** The median paired difference between matched buyer and seller price quotations is zero (i.e. self-reports are internally consistent).
- **H₀₄:** Payment-mode adoption is independent of actor class.
- **H₀₅:** There is no monotonic association between per-kilogram marketing cost and per-kilogram net profit at the retail tier.

H₀₂ and H₀₄ are stated for completeness; prior expectation, grounded in the cost structures documented in Chapter 2, is that H₀₂ will be rejected (strata differ systematically in cost and scale) while H₀₄ will not (adoption behaviour is a function of customer base, which varies within strata). These expectations are recorded before inspection and are not used to justify any analytical choice.

## 1.7 Scope and limitations of the study

The study is confined to **six markets in the Chattogram metropolitan region**, **ten commercially important marine fish species**, **120 respondents** (90 traders in three strata plus 30 consumers), and a **single-season cross-sectional window** in early March 2026. It measures prices, costs, margins, channel structure, payment behaviour and market infrastructure; it does not measure fish quality biologically, nutritional outcomes, or the economics of the fishing operation itself. Producer-side prices are proxied by first-sale quotations at the two landing-linked markets rather than by boat-level interviews with fishers — a proxy whose status is carried explicitly into every producer-share figure (Methodology §3.11c). The full statement of methodological limitations appears in Chapter 3, §3.11, and their bearing on interpretation is restated in Chapter 5, §5.5.

One further consideration bounds the survey window specifically. The collection period (2–7 March 2026) opens at the start of the annual national hilsa conservation ban, which in 2026 was reported to run **1 March – 30 April**, prohibiting the catching, selling, buying, transporting and holding of hilsa. Chattogram is **not** among the six designated hilsa sanctuary districts, so the local effect operates through supply and price rather than through a local closure. Because the precise terms and geographic reach of the annual notification should be confirmed against the Department of Fisheries circular before any seasonal claim is made, this study treats the ban as a **caveat on interpretation of the Ilish results** and not as a measured variable. It is stated here rather than left to be discovered in review.

## 1.8 Definition of key terms

| Term | Definition as used in this study |
|---|---|
| **Arat** | A wholesale fish trading yard where lots are assembled, weighed and auctioned under the management of its operator. |
| **Aratdar** | The operator of an *arat*; a commission-earning facilitator who provides space, weighing and auction coordination. He does **not** take ownership of the fish, and his income is commission or lease income, not a trading price spread. |
| **Bepari** | A mobile wholesaler who buys at landing points or upstream yards, transports, and sells through arats to retailers or other wholesalers. |
| **Faria** | A smaller-scale, shorter-distance variant of the bepari, often buying from and selling to other intermediaries. |
| **Khuchra (retailer)** | A vendor selling directly to final consumers from a fixed stall or vending spot within a market. |
| **Price spread** | Consumer price minus producer (first-sale) price, BDT per kilogram (eq. 3.6). |
| **Marketing margin (segment)** | The difference between a trader's selling and buying price for the same species-day quote, BDT per kilogram (eq. 3.4). |
| **Marketing cost (MC)** | All itemised cash expenses incurred by a trader, normalised to BDT per kilogram handled (eq. 3.3). |
| **Gross marketing margin (GMM %)** | Price spread as a percentage of the consumer price (eq. 3.7). |
| **Net marketing margin (NMM %)** | Price spread less total chain marketing cost, as a percentage of the consumer price (eq. 3.8). |
| **Producer's share (PS %)** | Producer (first-sale) price as a percentage of the consumer price (eq. 3.9). |
| **Marketing efficiency (ME)** | Shepherd-type ratio of output value to total marketing cost plus margins (eq. 3.10). |
| **MFS** | Mobile financial service — in Bangladesh practice, bKash, Nagad or similar phone-based payment. |
| **Maund** | Traditional unit of mass; converted throughout at **1 maund = 37.32 kg**. |
| **Chain-complete species** | A species with valid quotations at all four chain levels *and* at least three consumer-purchase observations; only such species enter pooled figures. |

## 1.9 Organisation of the paper

The paper comprises five chapters. **Chapter 1** (this chapter) sets out the background, the problem, the justification, the objectives, the research questions and the scope. **Chapter 2** reviews the literature on fish marketing channels, margins and producer shares in Bangladesh and identifies the gap the study addresses. **Chapter 3** describes the study area, the research design, the sampling procedure, the instruments, the measurement of variables, the analytical framework and the statistical methods, together with the quality-assurance, ethical and limitations provisions. **Chapter 4** presents and discusses the results in eleven sections, from respondent characteristics through the price chain, marketing costs, inter-market price variation, payment behaviour, marketing problems, infrastructure and consumer behaviour, to data quality and an explicit statement of what the chapter does and does not establish. **Chapter 5** summarises the findings against the objectives, sets out conclusions, recommends measures for policy and market management, and identifies directions for further research.
