## 2.1 Conceptual framework

The analytical vocabulary used throughout this study follows the classic agricultural-marketing tradition. Kohls and Uhl (1980) define the **price spread** as the difference between what the consumer pays and what the producer receives, and decompose it into the margins of the intermediaries plus the marketing costs they incur; Shepherd's (1965) efficiency ratio and the producer-side formulation of Acharya and Agarwal (1987) translate that decomposition into summary indices. The **producer's share** (PS%) — producer price expressed as a percentage of the consumer price — remains the most widely reported summary statistic because it can be derived from two prices alone and is comparable across studies and commodities. In Bangladesh fish-marketing studies these ratios are almost always reported for the auction-mediated channel in which *aratdars* (commission agents operating the wholesale *arats*), *beparis/farias* (mobile wholesalers), and *khuchra* retailers form the backbone between landing centres and consumers.

## 2.2 Producer's share and margins in Bangladesh fish marketing

The closest and most recent peer-reviewed benchmark is **Jahan et al. (2024)**, who analysed the marketing margin and profit structure of nine commercially important marine fish species of the southeast coast of Bangladesh (Cox's Bazar primary market through Dhaka secondary and consumer markets, 2001–2022 data series). They report that marketing margins and profits are systematically higher in long-chain (inland) channels than in short chains close to the landing centre, that secondary- and consumer-market margins have risen over two decades while primary-market margins fell, and that aratdar commissions run at **3–6%** of lot value — figures directly comparable with the auction-margin estimates of the present survey (Jahan, Chad, Hossain & Kamal, 2024, *Journal of Agribusiness and Rural Development*, 74(4); https://doi.org/10.17306/J.JARD.2024.00001). Earlier work on the **sea bass value chain in Bagerhat** reported a total marketing margin of 26% of the consumer price, with fishermen receiving about 74% of the final price through the primary market, and higher effective shares in shorter channels. At the other extreme, the multi-stage **hilsa chain from the Patuakhali coast to Dhaka retail markets** leaves the fishermen with roughly 55% of the consumer price, the largest single share accruing to wholesalers. A Cox's Bazar-based study of commercially important marine species placed fishermen's shares in the range 51–76% with an average near 59%. Studies of *loitta* (Bombay duck) and shrimp chains historically report marketing margins around 25–30% of the consumer price (Islam et al., 2006; Ahsan, 2016), implying producer shares of roughly 70–75% in relatively short coastal chains — figures that bracket the 68.9% pooled share estimated for the present Chattogram survey (Table 10). The recurring gradient is that **producer shares fall as the channel lengthens**: landing-linked city chains of the kind studied here (fisher → arat → retailer → consumer within one urban economy) typically leave 70–75% with the fisherman, whereas five-or-more-stage national chains (landing → district wholesale → Dhaka wholesale → *paiker* → retailer) can compress the fisherman's share towards a quarter of the Dhaka retail price — e.g., the September 2025 Bangladesh Trade and Tariff Commission report on the Dhaka hilsa chain. This study's design (Section 3.1) deliberately samples *within* Chattogram, so its producer shares should be compared with landing-city benchmarks, not with inland terminal-market chains; the Discussion (Chapter 4) states this explicitly to pre-empt a misleading comparison.

## 2.3 Market structure, problems and digital payments

Structural studies emphasise that informal, spatially fragmented markets lengthen chains: Rajbari research found marine fish (croaker, hilsa) arriving from Cox's Bazar, Chattogram and Khulna generated some of the highest margins precisely because several transport stages and trader categories intervene, while traders cited unplanned market location, poor drainage and high transport cost as the principal constraints (Heliyon, 2022) — a problem ranking closely resembling the present survey's top complaints (transport cost increase, lean-season low landings, monsoon waterlogging; Table 6). Price levels themselves are volatile: hilsa prices in Bangladesh rose roughly 29% year-on-year by September 2025 (Tk 900–2,200/kg at Dhaka retail, TCB data) and ~57% over five years amid supply shortfalls (Daily Star, September 2025; BTTC, September 2025), which underlines why the March 2026 survey window is a single-season snapshot and why the Discussion is restricted to the survey period. On the demand side, digital payment adoption has advanced rapidly: registered mobile financial service (MFS) accounts exceeded 210 million by FY2025 and cash's share of formal retail transactions had fallen to roughly one-third, with interoperable Bangla QR rollout ongoing (QR-payment adoption review, 2025; MFS impact assessment, WJAETS, 2025). These national figures provide the benchmark against which the down-chain gradient of MFS use observed in Chattogram fish markets (about 15% of Aratdar receipts vs 27% of retailer receipts; Table 5) can be judged as consistent with — though still behind — broader retail digitalisation.

## 2.4 Statistical methods used in comparable studies

Because fish prices in informal markets rarely satisfy normality assumptions, comparable studies rely on distribution-free procedures. The present methodology pre-registers Kruskal–Wallis H with Dunn post-hoc under Holm step-down control for inter-market price comparisons, Mann–Whitney U for stratum margin contrasts, Wilcoxon signed-rank for matched buyer–seller pairs, chi-square with sparse-cell fallback for payment-mode adoption, and Spearman's rank correlation for the marketing-cost–profit relationship (Methodology V4, Table 3.6; Kruskal and Wallis, 1952; Dunn, 1964; Holm, 1979). Wilcoxon signed-rank validation of matched *buy-versus-sell* self-reports — the pair-linking design also used here — has been recommended as a quality screen in market surveys of this type, with the caveat that the number of genuinely matched pairs is usually small, so the test should be read as a diagnostic rather than a powered hypothesis test.

## 2.5 Research gap addressed by this study

Two gaps recur in the literature. First, most recent marine-fish margin studies in Bangladesh are anchored on a single landing point (Cox's Bazar, Bagerhat) or on Dhaka's terminal markets; **Chattogram — the country's principal marine fish trading hub — is comparatively under-represented in published margin decomposition studies** despite hosting landing-linked wholesale arats (Fishery Ghat, Patenga) alongside dense retail markets within one urban system, which permits first-sale and consumer prices to be observed in a single survey frame. Second, published work rarely validates trader-quoted margins against matched transaction pairs and consumer exit slips. This study contributes a six-market, four-stratum price chain for ten commercial marine species in which every margin estimate is chain-anchored (first-sale auction price at landing-linked markets → wholesale → consumer-paid retail), internally additive by construction, and screened by matched-pair and consumer-slip checks — the design detailed in Chapter 3 and quantified in Chapter 4.
