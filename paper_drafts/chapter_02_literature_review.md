## 2.1 Conceptual framework

The analytical vocabulary used throughout this study follows the classic agricultural-marketing tradition. Kohls and Uhl (1980) define the **price spread** as the difference between what the consumer pays and what the producer receives, and decompose it into the margins of the intermediaries plus the marketing costs they incur; Shepherd's (1965) efficiency ratio and the producer-side formulation of Acharya and Agarwal (1987) translate that decomposition into summary indices. The **producer's share** (PS%) — producer price expressed as a percentage of the consumer price — remains the most widely reported summary statistic because it can be derived from two prices alone and is comparable across studies and commodities. In Bangladesh fish-marketing studies these ratios are almost always reported for the auction-mediated channel in which *aratdars* (commission agents operating the wholesale *arats*), *beparis/farias* (mobile wholesalers), and *khuchra* retailers form the backbone between landing centres and consumers.

## 2.2 Producer's share and margins in Bangladesh fish marketing

Empirical results from Bangladesh are heterogeneous, and the heterogeneity is itself informative: producer shares tend to fall as channel length and perishability risk rise, and to rise when a direct (fisher → retailer → consumer) channel is used. For marine species, recent work on the **sea bass value chain in Bagerhat** reported a total marketing margin of 26% of the consumer price, with fishermen receiving about 74% of the final price when selling through the primary (landing) market, and higher effective shares in shorter channels (marketing-channel assessment, J. of the Sylhet Agricultural Univ. system, 2023). At the other extreme, the multi-stage **hilsa chain from the Patuakhali coast to Dhaka retail markets** leaves the fishermen with roughly 55% of the consumer price once wholesalers and retailers take their margins, the largest single share accruing to wholesalers (value-chain analysis, coastal Bangladesh). A Cox's Bazar-based study of commercially important marine species placed fishermen's shares in the range 51–76% with an average near 59%, emphasising that species composition and the number of intermediaries drive the between-species differences (2022). Studies of *loitta* (Bombay duck) and shrimp chains historically report marketing margins around 25–30% of the consumer price (Islam et al., 2006; Ahsan, 2016), implying producer shares of roughly 70–75% in relatively short coastal chains — figures that bracket the 70.9% pooled share estimated for the present Chattogram survey (Table 10).

Commission evidence lines up well with the auction design of this study: aratdar commissions in marine fish landing and wholesale markets are commonly reported at **3–6%** of lot value, occasionally reaching 10% in credit-tied (*dadon*) arrangements, and the income ranking across intermediaries (wholesalers > beparies > aratdars > fishers) is consistently reproduced (marketing-margin study of the southeast Bay of Bengal fisheries, 2023). Retail margins for marine fish in Bangladesh are usually the largest single segment margin in absolute per-kg terms, typically 10–20% of the consumer price for fresh, ice-touched product, consistent with high spoilage risk and stall-level costs (Rajbari market-channel study, Heliyon, 2022).

## 2.3 Market structure, problems and digital payments

Structural studies emphasise that informal, spatially fragmented markets lengthen chains: Rajbari research found marine fish (croaker, hilsa) arriving from Cox's Bazar, Chattogram and Khulna generated some of the highest margins precisely because several transport stages and trader categories intervene, while traders cited unplanned market location, poor drainage and high transport cost as the principal constraints (Heliyon, 2022) — a problem ranking closely resembling the present survey's top complaints (spoilage, supply syndicate, frozen-import competition; Table 6). On the demand side, digital payment adoption has advanced rapidly in Bangladesh: registered mobile financial service (MFS) accounts exceeded 210 million by FY2025 and cash's share of formal retail transactions had fallen to roughly one-third, with interoperable Bangla QR rollout ongoing (QR-payment adoption review, 2025; MFS impact assessment, WJAETS, 2025). These national figures provide the benchmark against which the down-chain gradient of MFS use observed in Chattogram fish markets (about 14% of Aratdar receipts vs 29% of retailer receipts; Table 5) can be judged as consistent with — though still behind — broader retail digitalisation.

## 2.4 Statistical methods used in comparable studies

Because fish prices in informal markets rarely satisfy normality assumptions, comparable studies rely on distribution-free procedures. The present methodology pre-registers Kruskal–Wallis H with Dunn post-hoc under Holm step-down control for inter-market price comparisons, Mann–Whitney U for stratum margin contrasts, Wilcoxon signed-rank for matched buyer–seller pairs, chi-square with sparse-cell fallback for payment-mode adoption, and Spearman's rank correlation for the marketing-cost–profit relationship (Methodology V4, Table 3.6; Kruskal and Wallis, 1952; Dunn, 1964; Holm, 1979). Wilcoxon signed-rank validation of matched *buy-versus-sell* self-reports — the pair-linking design also used here — has been recommended as a quality screen in market surveys of this type, with the caveat that the number of genuinely matched pairs is usually small, so the test should be read as a diagnostic rather than a powered hypothesis test.

## 2.5 Research gap addressed by this study

Two gaps recur in the literature. First, most recent marine-fish margin studies in Bangladesh are anchored on a single landing point (Cox's Bazar, Bagerhat) or on Dhaka's terminal markets; **Chattogram — the country's principal marine fish trading hub — is comparatively under-represented in published margin decomposition studies** despite hosting landing-linked wholesale arats (Fishery Ghat, Patenga) alongside dense retail markets within one urban system, which permits first-sale and consumer prices to be observed in a single survey frame. Second, published work rarely validates trader-quoted margins against matched transaction pairs and consumer exit slips. This study contributes a six-market, four-stratum price chain for ten commercial marine species in which every margin estimate is chain-anchored (first-sale auction price at landing-linked markets → wholesale → consumer-paid retail), internally additive by construction, and screened by matched-pair and consumer-slip checks — the design detailed in Chapter 3 and quantified in Chapter 4.
