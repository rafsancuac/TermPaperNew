## 5.1 Summary of the study

The study set out to analyse the marketing system of ten commercially important marine fish species in the Chattogram metropolitan region and to establish how the consumer's taka is divided between the fisher and the intermediaries who stand between him and the market. Chapter 1 framed the problem: marine capture supplies only **12.53 % of Bangladesh's 50.18 lakh MT of fish production** and is the one segment that **declined in 2023–24 (6.29 lakh MT, −7.47 %)** (DoF, 2024), so the efficiency of the channel that carries it matters disproportionately to both fisher livelihoods and urban food costs.

Chapter 3 specified a **descriptive-cum-analytical cross-sectional survey** of six markets — two landing-linked (Fishery Ghat, Patenga) and four city retail markets — covering **120 respondents** (30 aratdars, 30 bepari/faria, 30 khuchra retailers, 30 exit-intercepted consumers) over a pilot on 2 March and collection on 3–7 March 2026. Five coordinated instruments (Forms A, B, R, M, C) captured prices, costs, channel linkages, payment behaviour, market infrastructure and consumer-side price verification. The analytical framework normalised marketing cost to the kilogram for **all three strata** (eq. 3.3), decomposed the price spread into segment margins (eq. 3.4–3.5), and computed gross and net marketing margins, the producer's share and Shepherd-type efficiency indices both consumer- and producer-referenced (eq. 3.6–3.10).

Chapter 4 reported the results. This chapter draws them together against the nine objectives of §1.4, states the conclusions they support, and sets out recommendations.

Six findings deserve emphasis because they carry beyond their own tables.

**First, the fisher's share is high by the standard of the literature, and the retail tier — not the wholesaler — is the largest single claimant.** At **72.4 %** the producer's share sits at the upper end of the 51–76 % band reported for Bangladesh marine chains (Chapter 2, §2.2), consistent with a short landing-city chain rather than a multi-stage inland one. Within the remaining 27.6 %, the retailer takes **12.8 %**, the bepari/faria **11.5 %**, and the aratdar only **3.3 %** — and the aratdar's small share is expected, since his income is commission for facilitation, not a trading spread (§3.3.1).

**Second, roughly half of the gross margin is consumed by marketing cost.** GMM of 27.6 % falls to an **NMM of 12.7 %** once the 136.19 BDT/kg of chain-wide cost is deducted. A study that measured margins alone would have overstated intermediaries' net gain by more than a factor of two. This is the single strongest argument for the per-kilogram cost normalisation of §3.7.1.

**Third, the pooled figure conceals a composition effect that matters.** At species level NMM ranges from **18.9 % for Ilish down to −2.2 % for Churi**. The negative value is not an error: for a low-value, high-volume species, charges levied as a percentage of lot value or per maund exceed the entire price spread, so Churi is effectively carried by the high-value species in the same basket. Any policy that adjusts value-proportional charges needs to see this, and only the per-stratum cost normalisation reveals it.

**Fourth, price dispersion is real but localised, not city-wide.** Five of six testable species showed significant inter-market variation, yet only four pairwise contrasts survived Holm control — and three of those four pitted a landing-linked market against Bahaddarhat, the outlying residential market. Ilish ranged from 1,337 BDT/kg at Fishery Ghat to 1,519 at Bahaddarhat, a **14 % spread**. The pattern is a geography-of-access effect, not a uniform differential that would imply widespread arbitrage failure.

**Fifth, digital payment has arrived at the consumer end but has not differentiated the traders.** The share of *receipts* settled through MFS rises monotonically down-chain, and 43.3 % of consumer transactions were already digital. Yet MFS *use frequency* was statistically independent of actor class (χ² p = 0.647, Cramér's V = 0.118). The correct reading is that adoption behaviour is uniform across strata while receipt composition differs, because retailers face a customer base that pays by phone. Conflating the two variables — a common error — would have produced a spurious "retailers are more digital" conclusion.

**Sixth, the weakest infrastructure sits exactly where the producer price is set.** Fishery Ghat and Patenga — the two markets that supply the first-sale price on which every producer-share figure depends — had **poor sanitation, irregular electricity and only partial roofing**, while the four city markets had regular power, complete or good roofing and medium-to-good sanitation. This is the physical context for the ice, salt and spoilage costs that dominate the traders' own problem ranking.

## 5.3 Conclusions

On the evidence of the analysed dataset, and within the bounds stated in §5.5:

1. **The Chattogram marine fish channel, as configured here, is moderately efficient and does not extract an unusual rent from the fisher.** A producer's share of 72.4 %, a Shepherd efficiency index above unity on both the consumer-referenced (2.35) and producer-referenced (1.70) forms, and a net marketing margin of 12.7 % together describe a chain in which more of the final price remains as product value than is consumed by marketing services.

2. **The margin that is largest in percentage terms is the one furthest from the landing, and it is partly a cost-recovery margin rather than pure rent.** The retailer's 12.8 % becomes a net profit of about **67 BDT/kg**, roughly **9.3 %** of the mean price consumers paid and about **57 %** of his gross margin, after ice, rent, washing, water and an average **5.5 % spoilage loss** are charged against it.

3. **Intermediation is heterogeneous, and the aratdar is not the intermediary he is popularly taken to be.** His 3.3 % share of the consumer's taka, levied as commission on a large throughput (801 kg/day), is an order of magnitude below the trading margins on either side of him. Policy aimed at "eliminating middlemen" would, on this evidence, be aimed at the wrong actor.

4. **The binding constraints reported by traders are physical and seasonal, not financial.** Price volatility, thin lean-season landings and the cost of ice and salt outranked transport and tolls. Two of the five most-cited problems concern ice and salt directly, and the infrastructure audit independently located the worst conditions at the two landing-linked markets. Capital is available — credit is universal — but cold chain is not.

5. **Information, not intermediation, is the most plausible source of the observed price dispersion.** A 14 % Ilish spread between a landing market and an outlying residential market, in a city where prices are not publicly posted and transactions are bilateral and credit-mediated, is more consistent with an information gap than with market power exercised uniformly.

6. **Negative net margins on low-value species are a structural feature of value-proportional charging.** Charging commission as a percentage of lot value and tolls per maund is neutral with respect to a trader's costs but regressive with respect to the species mix, and it makes the cheap-protein species — the ones that matter most for food security — the least attractive to handle.

## 5.4 Recommendations

Each recommendation is tied to the finding that motivates it. They are directed at the Department of Fisheries, the market committees and lease-holders responsible for the *arats*, and the financial-service providers active in these markets.

**R1 — Publish daily reference prices at landing and retail (from Finding 4, Conclusion 5).** Post the previous day's first-sale price by species at each market entrance and at the retail rows, and disseminate the same figure by SMS or a simple board at Fishery Ghat and Patenga. A visible first-sale anchor narrows the information gap that the 14 % Ilish dispersion implies, and it costs almost nothing.

**R2 — Prioritise infrastructure investment at the two landing-linked markets (from Finding 6, Conclusion 4).** Fishery Ghat and Patenga need reliable electricity, drainage, complete roofing and functioning sanitation before the city markets do. They are where the producer price is set, where spoilage originates, and — on the audit evidence — where conditions are worst.

**R3 — Expand ice capacity and cold chain at the point of landing (from Finding 6, Conclusion 4).** Ice and salt account for two of the five most-cited problems, and spoilage makes a third within the top ten. Cooperative or subsidised flake-ice plants sited at the landing points, plus insulated containers for the bepari's onward haul, attack the cost item that traders themselves rank highest and that the negative NMM for low-value species makes most damaging.

**R4 — Formalise the trade credit that already runs the channel (from Finding 2).** No retailer in the sample sold entirely for cash, and 83.3 % sold some or almost all on credit. Rather than attempting to displace it, link it to formal working capital: MFS-based trade-credit products, microfinance lines tied to *arat* throughput records, and a simple receivables register. Delayed credit recovery was cited by 23.3 % of traders.

**R5 — Extend digital payment and use the transaction record (from Finding 5).** With 43.3 % of consumer transactions already digital, the conditions for Bangla QR acceptance at retail stalls are present. Beyond the payment convenience, a digital record converts an invisible market into a measurable one and gives the Department of Fisheries a price series it currently lacks.

**R6 — Do not target the aratdar; support shorter channels for high-value species instead (from Conclusion 3).** The aratdar's 3.3 % is a facilitation fee, not a rent. Where the scope for raising the producer's share exists, it is in shortening the chain for the high-value species: periodic fisher-to-consumer *haats*, direct-supply arrangements with hotels and institutional buyers, and cooperatives that let fishing groups sell above the landing auction.

**R7 — Review value-proportional charges against the low-value species mix (from Finding 3, Conclusion 6).** Where charges are levied as a percentage of lot value or per maund, they fall hardest on the cheapest fish. Weight-based or value-tiered tolling, or a concession for the low-price tier, would remove the disincentive to handle the species that contribute most to affordable protein.

**R8 — Institutionalise routine price monitoring with this instrument (from Conclusion 5, §3.13).** The Form A/Form C pairing — first-sale price at the landing markets, realised price from consumer exit slips — is cheap to administer monthly and yields a producer-share series. Run as a standing DoF observation at two landing and four retail markets, it would give Bangladesh the marine-fish price series it does not presently have.

## 5.5 Limitations and directions for further research

The limitations stated in §3.11 apply to everything above and are not repeated in full. Four of them most constrain the conclusions.

**Seasonality is unmeasured.** A single cross-sectional window in early March cannot distinguish a structural margin from a seasonal one, and the window opens at the start of the annual hilsa conservation ban (§1.7), which plausibly affects Ilish availability and price. **A repeat survey in the peak and lean seasons, with the ban window treated explicitly as a factor, is the single most valuable extension of this work.**

**The producer price is a proxy.** P<sub>p</sub> is the first-sale auction quote at the landing-linked markets, not a boat-level transaction. If fishermen receive less than the arat records — through deductions, weighing practices or pre-arranged sales — the 72.4 % figure is an upper bound. **Boat-level interviews and direct observation of auction settlement would test this directly and should be the next study's priority.**

**Loss is self-reported.** Spoilage and transit damage rest on respondent recall. The 5.5 % retailer spoilage figure drives a material part of the retail cost structure. **Objective loss measurement — weighment at purchase and at sale — would replace recall with observation.**

**Inter-market inference is exploratory.** No species met the pre-registered n ≥ 5 per-cell rule, so the Kruskal–Wallis and Dunn results are descriptive of this sample. **A design with more quotations per market cell, or fewer markets with deeper coverage, would convert the RQ1 tests from exploratory to confirmatory.**

Three further directions suggest themselves from the findings themselves. **Value-chain finance** — how credit is priced, collateralised and recovered across the chain — is barely studied and is evidently central, given that the channel runs on it. **Consumer willingness to pay for quality assurance** follows from the finding that purity, not price, is the dominant reason for choosing a seller. And a **comparative study across landing cities** (Chattogram, Cox's Bazar, Khulna) using this identical instrument would establish whether the 72.4 % share is a Chattogram property or a coastal one.

## 5.6 What this chapter establishes, and what changes with real data

Because the analysed dataset is synthetic, this chapter establishes **the completeness and internal coherence of the reporting chain**, not any proposition about Chattogram's markets. It shows that the nine objectives of §1.4 are each answerable from the instrument as designed; that the pipeline produces every indicator the methodology specifies — including the net-margin and efficiency measures that the literature often omits — and that it does so with the accounting identities intact (segment margins telescoping to the spread; producer's share and spread summing to 100; Table 3's pooled share equalling Table 10's); that the inferential plan of Table 3.6 is executable end to end; and that the two independent engines agree on 22 of 23 tables.

When real field data are collected, **the conclusions in §5.3 and the recommendations in §5.4 must be re-derived, not merely re-labelled**. Each of the five conclusions is a claim about magnitudes — 72.4 %, 12.7 %, 12.8 %, 67 BDT/kg, 14 % — and every one of those numbers will move. The procedure is mechanical: fill the yellow cells of `Marine_Fish_Marketing_Data_Entry_Chattogram_REAL_EMPTY.xlsx`, run `verify_filled.py` to 24/24, then the analysis chain and `scripts/R/MS499_full_analysis.R`, and rewrite §5.2 from the regenerated tables. What will carry over unchanged is the **structure** of Table 5.1, the **logic** linking each finding to its recommendation, and the **candidate explanations** catalogued in §5.3 — any of which the new data may confirm, reverse, or render moot.
