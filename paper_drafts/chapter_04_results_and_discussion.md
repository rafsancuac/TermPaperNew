## 4.1 Socio-economic profile of respondents

The survey covered 120 respondents — 30 aratdars, 20 beparis and 10 farias, 30 retailers and 30 exit-intercepted consumers — across six markets during 3–7 March 2026 (Table 1). Traders were, on average, 41.0 years old (±10.9) with 16.0 years (±9.0) in the marine-fish trade. Aratdars were the most experienced (mean age 44.1; 18.7 years in business) and had the largest average family size (6.1); retailers were the youngest group (38.8 years; 12.8 years in business). Education was dominated by primary and secondary levels: among all traders 33.3% had primary and 35.6% secondary schooling, while only 14.4% reached higher secondary. Notably, 23.3% of aratdars and 23.3% of retailers were illiterate, compared with none among the (larger-lot) bepari group (Chart C4). The profile is consistent with a mature, informally schooled trading population in which the more capital-intensive wholesaling roles (aratdar, bepari) concentrate older and more experienced operators.

## 4.2 Business scale and channel structure

Reported daily volume handled falls steeply along the chain (Table 2; Chart C7): aratdars averaged 797 kg/day (median 821; range 373–1,306), bepari/faria 205 kg/day (median 187), and retailers 100 kg/day (median 104; SD 27). Aratdars employed on average 5.4 workers at a mean daily wage of 637 BDT. Bepari/faria made on average 1.9 trips per period using 80.0 kg of ice per day; retailers reported 5.4% spoilage on average.

Within the intermediate stratum, 20 respondents (66.7%) identified as bepari and 10 (33.3%) as faria; 83.3% traded year-round (Table 2b). Supply sources confirm the auction-centred geography of the channel: 43.3% of bepari/faria reported buying directly from fishermen and 40.0% from aratdars, with source locations split between local landing points (Fishery Ghat landing, Karnaphuli river ghat) and coastal origins (Bhola charter landing, Cox's Bazar, Kutubdia, Moheshkhali, Teknaf, Kuakata). On the sell side 56.7% of retailers bought from aratdars (16.7% from farias, 16.7% directly from fishermen) and 70.0% sold to households, 10.0% to hawkers and 10.0% to hotels. Credit is pervasive: 70.0% of bepari/faria and 60.0% of retailers make 'some' credit sales, while 16.7% of retailers extend credit on almost all sales.

## 4.3 Price chain and margins — the core result

Table 3 reports the per-species price ladder built on chain-consistent level means. Producer (first-sale) prices are Form A purchase quotes at the two landing-linked markets (M1 Fishery Ghat, M6 Patenga) — the points where an aratdar's buy price is the net auction price paid to the fisherman; wholesale prices are Bepari/Faria sell quotes across markets; and the retail anchor is the price consumers actually paid (Form C exit slips). Segment margins are differences of consecutive level means, so they telescope exactly to the total spread, and margins plus the producer's share sum to 100% of the consumer taka by construction.

Ilish, the flagship species, illustrates the ladder: fisherman ≈1,032 BDT/kg at landing → auction 1,083 → bepari sale 1,236 → consumer 1,420 (retailer vendor quotes averaged 1,487, i.e. quoted prices exceed realised prices by ≈4.7% — bargaining margin). The pooled producer's share of the consumer price is **70.9%** (Table 10), meaning 29.1% of the consumer taka (246 BDT/kg on average) is absorbed between landing and the consumer's hand: auction spread 27.8 BDT/kg (3.3% of the consumer price), bepari/faria wholesale margin 99.6 BDT/kg (11.7%), and the retail margin to consumers 119.0 BDT/kg (14.0%) — the single largest segment (Chart C2). Producer shares were fairly uniform across species with adequate data, ranging from 68.3% (Poa) and 68.8% (Rupchanda) to 74.7% (Koral), with no monotonic relationship to price tier: the species with the thinnest per-kg spreads (e.g. Loitta, 78.6) still returned shares near 70%, because margins scale with absolute price (Table 3). The low-volume S08 Kankoita and S10 Harina each rest on a single consumer-paid quote (Table 3, Consumer_paid_n=1), so their producer shares are **not reported** rather than presented as fragile estimates.

Two qualifications are methodological and should be stated. First, chain statistics pool **chain-complete species only** — "complete" requires all four chain stages observed *and* at least three consumer-paid quotes; eight species qualify (S01–S07 and S09). Harina (S10) is excluded because no landing-market auction sell quote was observed, and Kankoita (S08) because its consumer price rests on a single exit slip; both remain available for descriptive price reporting (Tables 11/13) with blank share cells. Loitta (S09) enters the pooled statistics although its retail quotes cover only two markets (flagged in Table 3's Reporting_status). Second, the aggregate 'ALL' row is the unweighted mean of species-level means (composition-consistent), not a volume-weighted average.

## 4.4 Prices by market and species

Retailer selling prices by species and market (Table 11; Chart C6 heatmap) reveal a consistent city-centre gradient: for Ilish, mean retail quotes ranged from 1,416 BDT/kg at Fishery Ghat to 1,571 at Bahaddarhat (Chawkbazar 1,480; Kazir Dewri 1,491; Karnaphuli Complex 1,497; Patenga 1,445), i.e. about 11% between the cheapest and dearest markets. Rupchanda shows the same ordering (Bahaddarhat 1,717 vs Fishery Ghat 1,586). Three low-tier species were too thinly quoted to support anything beyond description — Kankoita in 2 markets (Fishery Ghat 310; Patenga 340), Loitta in 2 (Fishery Ghat 247; Karnaphuli 279) and Harina in a single market (Karnaphuli Complex 230) — and are accordingly flagged **descriptive-only in Table 3 (Reporting_status)** under Methodology §3.8's rule for species appearing in fewer than three markets. Because Kankoita's and Harina's consumer-paid prices each rest on a single exit slip (Consumer_paid_n=1), their producer shares are not reported at all; Loitta's share (70.6%, n=6 consumer slips) is retained but carries the market-coverage caveat.

Per-market wholesale–retail decomposition (Table 13; Chart C8) shows the retail margin is broadly stable in percentage terms across markets, with quote-based margins systematically higher than consumer-paid (realised) margins — the retail bargaining gap noted above. Kruskal–Wallis tests across markets (retailer sell series) were significant at the exploratory cell threshold (n≥3 in ≥3 markets) for Ilish (H=10.72, p=0.030), Rupchanda (H=11.16, p=0.048), Lakkha (H=6.12, p=0.047), Surma (H=12.20, p=0.016) and Churi (H=6.89, p=0.032), and marginal for Koral (H=7.74, p=0.052) (Table 14). After Dunn post-hoc with Holm control, only the Fishery Ghat–Bahaddarhat Ilish contrast remained significant (p=0.012), implying that the omnibus signals are driven mostly by the landing-market vs city-market gap rather than by differences among the four city retail markets (Table 14b). No species met the pre-specified n≥5-per-cell rule in this dataset — a coverage constraint of the five-respondent-per-cell design that must be acknowledged (see Section 4.10 and recommendations).

## 4.5 Marketing costs

Mean cost items by actor are reported in Table 4. Among percent-basis aratdars (n=16) the auction commission averaged **3.34% of lot value** (SD 1.06); bepari/faria paid a near-identical 3.62% to aratdars on average (n=26; flat-rate payers, n=4, paid 700 BDT/lot) — mutually consistent and in line with the 3–6% commission band reported in the literature. Aratdar rents averaged 184,444 BDT/year (yearly payers, n=18) or 19,583 BDT/month (n=12); electricity bills averaged 8,331 BDT/month (n=26). Bepari/faria transport cost 1,637 BDT per trip with loading/unloading at 258 BDT/trip, ice use of 80.0 kg/day (259 BDT/day), and self-reported transit damage of 4.8%. Retailers paid 205 BDT/day stall rent, 185 BDT/day for ice and 80 BDT/day for washing/water/other, with spoilage of 5.4% of purchases. Note that cost items mix daily/monthly/yearly/per-trip frequencies; per-kilogram marketing-cost normalisation is therefore only performed for retailers in Section 4.10 (see Table 17 note).

## 4.6 Payment modes

Receipts remain cash-dominant but digital payment rises systematically down-chain (Table 5; Chart C3): cash averaged 67.6% of aratdar receipts, 63.9% for bepari/faria and 55.2% for retailers; MFS (bKash/Nagad) averaged 13.6% → 15.7% → 28.6% across the same groups, and credit 18.8% → 20.4% → 16.1%. Retailer MFS-regular use is consistent with a customer base that pays by phone: among consumers intercepted, 36.7% paid by bKash, 36.7% cash, 20.0% Nagad app and 6.7% credit — i.e. **57% of consumer transactions were digital**. A chi-square test of MFS-use frequency (Regular/Occasional/Never) against actor class found no association (χ²=1.22, df=4, p=0.875; likelihood-ratio p=0.881; exact Fisher p=0.895; Cramér's V=0.08 — Table 15): adoption differences along the chain in mean receipt *shares* reflect transaction-size and counterparty mix rather than distinct adoption behaviour.

## 4.7 Marketing problems

Traders named 12 distinct problems across three mention slots (Table 6; Chart C5). The most frequently cited were **unsold-fish spoilage loss (31.1% of 90 traders)** and **supply-syndicate control (31.1%)**, followed by competition from frozen imported fish (28.9%), high ice price (26.7%), day-to-day price volatility (25.6%), low landings in the lean season (24.4%) and monsoon waterlogging (24.4%). Aratdars emphasised irregular electricity and load-shedding (33.3%) and spoilage (40.0%); retailers felt volatility most (33.3%). The twin spoilage/syndicate ranking echoes the Rajbari market-channel findings for marine fish and supports the infrastructure and transparency recommendations in Chapter 5.

## 4.8 Market infrastructure

Form M observations (Table 7) contrast the two landing-linked markets with the four city markets. Fishery Ghat (mixed wholesale-retail, 04:30–13:00, 210 stalls) has ice available but poor sanitation, irregular electricity and medium platforms; Patenga (65 stalls, 04:00–12:30) is similar with a landing-side basket fee (20–40 BDT/maund). The city retail markets — Chawkbazar (160 stalls), Kazir Dewri (85), Karnaphuli Complex (120) and Bahaddarhat (110) — are better served: regular electricity, complete platforms/roofing (Karnaphuli Complex rated Good throughout) and medium sanitation. Toll arrangements vary between Aratdar-fee (M1: 2.5–5% commission per lot), committee tolls (M2–M4, 40–60 BDT/stall/day) and mixed arrangements (M5).

## 4.9 Consumer behaviour

Consumers (n=30) split 53.3% weekly / 46.7% daily shoppers with a mean visit interval of 3.6 days (Table 8). Freshness/purity was the dominant reason for choosing a seller (33.3%), ahead of price (23.3%), proximity (23.3%), familiar shop (16.7%) and variety (3.3%). Of 69 focal-purchase records, 52 were purchases made on the interview day, 71.0% from fixed retailers and 4.3% from hawkers (the rest are 'not today' rows). Mean focal price paid was 699 BDT/kg and mean purchase size 1.57 kg. Non-focal purchases (Table 9) were dominated by other marine items — Khoira (n=5, 268 BDT/kg), Bagda shrimp (n=3, 1,033), Datina (n=3, 357), Moid (n=3, 217) — with single reports of crab, local lobster (2,615 BDT/kg), chatka shrimp and faisya.

## 4.10 Data-quality screens and statistical validation

**Normality screening.** As pre-registered (Methodology §3.8), Shapiro–Wilk screening was applied to the pooled price series before any test: every chain series proved strongly non-normal (W ≈ 0.83–0.91, p < 0.001 — Table 18), confirming the need for the distribution-free battery used throughout.

**Matched pairs (Wilcoxon).** Sixteen buyer–seller pairs were linked across the six markets (2–3 per market, Method 3.3.4); 14 pairs were complete (both sides numeric). The median paired difference (seller's sell − buyer's buy of the same lot) was 0.59 BDT/kg and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was non-significant (W=23, p=0.116 — Table 12b): self-reported chain prices are internally consistent, corroborating the margin structure built from level means.

**Stratum margins.** Per-respondent own-quote margins (sell − buy, Method 3.7.2) were 30.1 BDT/kg median for aratdars, 83.7 for bepari/faria and 182.0 for retailers; all three pairwise Mann–Whitney contrasts are highly significant (p<0.0001 — Table 16). Note these quote-based magnitudes exceed the consumer-anchored retail margin of Table 10 by design, because vendor quotes run ~2.4% above realised consumer prices (Table 3 ALL row).

**Cost–profit association.** For retailers, per-kg marketing cost (stall rent + ice + washing, daily, divided by daily capacity) averaged a few BDT/kg only (n=30) and correlated positively with net margin per kg (Spearman ρ=0.43, p=0.018 — Table 17): retailers facing higher daily fixed costs tend to quote higher margins, evidence of cost-plus pricing at the stall level.

**Statistical caveats.** (i) No species attained the pre-specified ≥5-quotes-per-market cell rule in every market; inter-market tests are therefore presented at an explicitly labelled exploratory threshold (n≥3), and species with thinner coverage (Poa, Kankoita, Loitta, Harina) are reported descriptively only, as Methodology §3.8 prescribes. (i-b) Every producer share and chain margin rests on the consumer-paid sample whose size is disclosed in Table 3 (Consumer_paid_n): species with a single slip (Kankoita, Harina) are suppressed entirely, and the two species with n=3 (Rupchanda, Lakkha) should be read with corresponding caution. (ii) All inferential statements are two-sided at α=0.05, and multiple-comparison control follows the Holm step-down. (iii) The Wilcoxon test is a consistency diagnostic on 14 pairs, not a powered test, as anticipated in the methodology. (iv) Because one expected cell of the payment-mode table fell below 5, Pearson chi-square (p=0.875) is reported alongside the likelihood-ratio test (p=0.881) and the exact Freeman–Halton/Fisher test (p=0.895); conclusions are identical under all three. (v) All computations were performed in Python 3 (pandas/NumPy/SciPy) with the analysis scripts retained in the repository for full reproducibility, and all price and margin statements are restricted to the 3–7 March 2026 survey window; given the sharp upward drift in marine-fish prices through 2025, the real-data collection round in March 2026 must be checked against prevailing landing prices before the numbers in this chapter are finalised.
